Health insurance for parents after 60: what changes

Premium is the visible cost. Co-payment, waiting periods and room-rent caps are the ones that decide whether the cover works.

Reviewed 15 November 2026

Buying health cover for a parent over 60 is a different exercise from buying it for yourself at 30. The premium is higher, the conditions are more likely to be pre-existing, and the policy design shifts in ways that are easy to miss until you claim.

1. Co-payment is standard, and it compounds

Most senior-citizen policies apply a co-payment — typically 10% to 20% of every claim. A 20% co-payment on a ₹4 lakh hospital bill leaves ₹80,000 with you. That is not a small print issue; it is the difference between cover and partial cover.

Some policies waive the co-payment for a higher premium. Run the arithmetic: if the waiver costs ₹12,000 a year and you claim once every three years, the waiver pays for itself on a single ₹2 lakh hospitalisation.

2. Pre-existing conditions and the waiting clock

Diabetes, hypertension and cardiac history are near-universal after 60. Most policies impose a waiting period of two to four years on them. During that window, related treatment is excluded — and "related" can be interpreted broadly.

Declare everything. Non-disclosure is the most common reason claims are rejected years later, and insurers can and do check hospital records from before the policy started.

3. Room rent caps punish more than the room

A 1% cap on a ₹5 lakh sum insured is ₹5,000 a day. Most policies apply a proportionate deduction to the whole bill when you exceed it, not just the room charge. In a metro hospital, that single clause can cut a claim by a third.

4. Renewal is not guaranteed forever

Regulators have pushed insurers away from lifetime renewability being discretionary, but the mechanics still matter: if you let a policy lapse, re-entry at 68 after a cardiac event is effectively impossible or extremely expensive. Set the renewal on autopay and treat it as non-negotiable.

5. The sum insured is a decision about your savings

A ₹5 lakh cover in a metro is thin. One cardiac procedure with a week in ICU can cross it. For parents, ₹10 lakh is closer to adequate, and a policy with restoration is worth more than a slightly larger headline sum without it.

What to do before you buy

  • Ask for the policy wording, not the brochure, and search it for four words: waiting, room, co-pay, sub-limit.
  • Check whether your parents' usual hospital is in the cashless network — a network list from the insurer, not the agent.
  • Compare the co-payment and waiting periods between two policies before comparing premiums. The cheaper premium is usually buying a worse claim.
  • Declare every condition, in writing, and keep the acknowledgement.

Five situations and what the policy does

A 66-year-old with diabetes, admitted for a foot infection. Diabetes is declared and the pre-existing wait is two years. If the policy is 30 months old, the claim is inside the wait and is examined for whether the infection was a complication of diabetes. Many policies exclude diabetic complications during the wait; some cover them after.

A 64-year-old needing a pacemaker. Cardiac procedures often carry their own sub-limit, or a separate waiting period, or both. The sum insured is irrelevant if the sub-limit is ₹1.5 lakh and the bill is ₹3.2 lakh.

A 70-year-old with a 20% co-payment, bill of ₹5 lakh. The insurer pays ₹4 lakh, the family pays ₹1 lakh — plus the non-covered consumables. The premium saving from choosing the co-payment policy was perhaps ₹6,000 a year.

A policy that lapses at 68. Re-entry is a new policy with new waiting periods on every pre-existing condition, at an older age, usually with a medical examination. Two years of cover are lost. This is the most expensive mistake in the whole category.

A parent admitted for a day-care procedure. Cataract, dialysis, chemotherapy — many are day-care now. Policies that only cover "hospitalisation exceeding 24 hours" will exclude them unless day-care is named.

The premium question, honestly

For a 65-year-old, ₹10 lakh of cover typically costs ₹35,000–₹55,000 a year depending on the insurer, city and co-payment. It rises with age, and it rises faster after 70.

Compare that with the alternative: a single cardiac procedure with a week in ICU in a metro hospital can cost ₹4–6 lakh. Two years of premium is roughly one hospitalisation. That is the entire financial argument for cover after 60, and it does not depend on whether you "get your money back".

If the premium is genuinely unaffordable, the honest move is a smaller sum insured with no co-payment, not a large sum insured with a 20% co-pay. The second looks like more cover and behaves like less.

Questions people ask

Can I add a parent to my own family floater? Some insurers allow it, sometimes only if the parent is under a certain age, and the whole floater's premium rises. A separate senior-citizen policy is usually cleaner.

Is a pre-existing condition uninsurable? No — it is excluded for a waiting period, then covered. The trap is non-disclosure, not the condition itself.

Do I need a medical test? Above 60, almost always. Declare what the test finds; the insurer cannot reject a claim for a condition it knew about at underwriting.

What if the parent is over 75? Fewer insurers, higher premiums, more exclusions. Start early — every year of delay narrows the options.

The family conversation

Most policies for parents are bought by adult children, and the decision usually involves money that is not entirely the buyer's. Two things are worth settling before you compare premiums: who pays the annual premium if it rises by 20% at 70, and who is the nominee. Both are easier to agree in a calm year than during a hospital admission.

A worked cost comparison

Policy A: ₹10 lakh, 20% co-payment, ₹38,000 premium. Policy B: ₹10 lakh, no co-payment, ₹52,000 premium. One hospitalisation of ₹4 lakh in the next five years:

A pays ₹3.2 lakh and the family pays ₹80,000. B pays ₹4 lakh. Over five years A costs ₹1.9 lakh in premium, B costs ₹2.6 lakh. A single admission wipes out A's saving and then some.

The arithmetic only works in favour of the cheaper premium if nobody is hospitalised — which is not a bet worth making on a parent over 65.

Three documents to keep

The proposal form you signed, the policy schedule, and the renewal receipts. At claim time the insurer will ask what was declared; the proposal form is the only record that settles it. Photograph all three and keep them in one folder that the whole family can reach.

Senior-citizen policies versus ordinary ones

A dedicated senior-citizen policy usually has a higher entry age, no upper age limit for renewal, shorter pre-existing waiting periods, and a co-payment. An ordinary policy taken earlier may have better terms but stops accepting new entrants at a certain age.

The practical rule: if a parent already has a policy, keep renewing it even if a newer one looks cheaper. Continuity of waiting periods is worth more than a small premium difference.

If the insurer declines

A decline is not the end. Options include a policy with a longer waiting period, a higher co-payment, a lower sum insured, or a dedicated condition-specific product. A common fallback is a fixed-benefit plan that pays a lump sum on specified diagnoses rather than reimbursing bills — less useful but obtainable when indemnity cover is not.

Whatever the route, get the reason for the decline in writing. It determines what to disclose to the next insurer, and non-disclosure of a previous decline is itself a ground for rejection later.
