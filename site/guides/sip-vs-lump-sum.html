SIP vs lump sum

Same fund, same money, very different experience. What each does to your risk, and which one suits which situation.

Illustrative arithmetic · no return is forecast

SIPLump sum
How it worksA fixed amount invested every monthEverything invested on one day
Price paidAverages out — some units bought high, some lowOne price, on one day
Timing riskSpread over monthsConcentrated on the day you invest
Cash neededA monthly commitmentThe full amount upfront
SuitsSalary income, building a habitA bonus, maturity or inheritance
Risk of stoppingSIPs get paused when money is tightNone — the money is already in

The arithmetic, with assumptions stated

₹10,000 a month for 10 years at a constant 12% a year grows to about ₹23.2 lakh, on ₹12 lakh invested. A single ₹1 lakh invested at the same 12% for 10 years becomes about ₹3.1 lakh.

Those are arithmetic results, not forecasts. No fund returns 12% every year, and the order of returns matters: a lump sum invested just before a 30% fall takes years to recover, while a SIP keeps buying through it.

What actually decides it

  • Do you have the money now, or does it arrive monthly? Most people do not have a lump sum to invest — the question answers itself.
  • How long until you need it? Under three years, neither structure fixes the real risk, which is equity itself.
  • Would you panic and redeem? A lump sum that falls 25% in month two tests that harder than a SIP.
  • Lump sum in a falling market is the case for staggering — spreading the entry over 6–12 months, which is a SIP with a fixed number of instalments.
