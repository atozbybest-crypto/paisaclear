The emergency fund: how much, and where to keep it in India

Before returns, before taxes, before any product comparison — the money that stops a bad month becoming a bad year.

Reviewed 15 November 2026

Every product comparison on this site assumes you have money you will not need for years. That assumption fails for most people, and it fails at the worst possible moment. The emergency fund is the piece of financial planning that makes every other piece possible.

How much

Three to six months of essential expenses. Not income — expenses. Rent or EMI, utilities, school fees, groceries, transport, insurance premiums, and the minimum on any debt. If your household spends ₹60,000 a month on essentials, the target is ₹1.8 lakh to ₹3.6 lakh.

Lean towards six months if your income is variable, if you are the only earner, if your job is in a cyclical industry, or if someone in the family has a condition that might need treatment. Lean towards three if you have two stable incomes and no dependants.

Where to keep it — three rules

1. It must be available in 24 hours. Not five years, not at maturity, not subject to a penalty that makes you think twice.

2. It must not fall in value. This money is not invested. A fund that can be down 15% in a bad month is the wrong home for the money you need after losing a job.

3. It must be separate from your spending account. If it sits in the account you use for groceries, it will quietly disappear on a good month and be missing on a bad one.

The realistic options

  • A separate savings account at a different bank, with autopay switched off. Lowest effort, lowest return — and that is fine, because this is insurance, not investment.
  • A sweep-in FD linked to savings. Slightly better rate, still instant. Watch the minimum balance rules and the tax on the interest.
  • A liquid or overnight mutual fund. Better return, one-day redemption. Acceptable if you understand that the NAV can dip slightly and that redemption is not instantaneous.
  • Not a fixed deposit with a penalty, not PPF, not an endowment policy, not equity. All four fail one of the three rules above.

The tax point nobody mentions

Interest on your emergency fund is taxed at your slab. At 30%, a 3% savings account yields about 2.1% after tax — below inflation, which is the correct trade. You are paying a small amount to avoid a large loss, exactly as you do with insurance.

Building it when you have nothing set aside

Start with one month of expenses. Automate a transfer on salary day, before the spending starts. Once you reach one month, aim for three, then six. It is slower than it sounds and faster than it feels.

Do this before you open a PPF account, before you start a SIP, and before you compare fixed deposit rates — because without it, the first emergency forces you to break whichever of those you chose, usually at the worst possible time.

What counts as an emergency

A useful test: an emergency is an expense that is unexpected, necessary and urgent — all three. A medical bill is all three. A school fee is necessary but expected, so it belongs in a monthly budget, not the emergency fund. A wedding is expected and not urgent. A salary delay is all three.

Writing your own list of what qualifies, before you need it, is the whole discipline. Without the list, the fund becomes a second spending account within a year.

Where people actually keep it, and what goes wrong

In the same savings account. Disappears quietly. The balance looks high, so spending rises to match it.

In a fixed deposit with a penalty. Available, but with a 0.5–1% penalty and a branch visit. The friction is small in normal times and significant in a crisis.

In equity. The fund is needed most in exactly the months when equity is down. This is the most common mistake among young earners, and the most expensive.

In PPF or an endowment policy. Locked. Both are savings products with a purpose, and neither is available when the emergency arrives.

In a relative's account. Sounds safe; is not. Availability is not guaranteed at 2 a.m. on a Sunday.

Building it on a real salary

Suppose take-home is ₹80,000 and essentials are ₹55,000. One month of cover is ₹55,000. At ₹10,000 a month, three months takes seventeen months — too slow to feel real.

Two accelerations: put the annual bonus in first, and route any windfall — a tax refund, a gift — straight to the fund before it reaches spending. Most people reach three months in under a year that way, and the automated monthly transfer finishes it.

Questions people ask

Should I build the emergency fund before paying off a loan? Yes, one month of expenses first — otherwise the next emergency goes on a credit card at 36% a year. Then clear high-interest debt, then finish the fund.

Do I need a bigger fund if I am self-employed? Six to twelve months. Income variability, not expenses, is what the fund is insuring.

Should it be in a joint account? Yes, if both partners need access. One account, both names, no debit card in regular use.

What if I use it? That is what it is for. Rebuild it on the same schedule — automated, monthly, before spending — and treat the rebuild as urgent as the original saving.

Does it need to grow? No. Its job is to be there, not to compound. A fund that grows 6% but is locked fails; a fund that earns 3% and is available succeeds.

Where to hold it, worked through

₹3 lakh of emergency fund, held in a savings account at 3%: ₹9,000 a year before tax. In a liquid fund at 6.5%: about ₹19,500 — roughly ₹10,500 more, at the cost of one-day redemption and a NAV that can dip slightly.

Is ₹10,500 worth the friction? For most households, yes — provided the fund is genuinely liquid and genuinely separate. For a household with irregular income and no other savings, the certainty of the savings account is worth more than the extra interest.

The three-month test

If you lost your income tomorrow, how many months could you cover essentials without borrowing or selling anything? Count only money available within 48 hours, in cash or a bank account. That number is your real emergency cover, whatever your total savings say.

Most people discover the number is smaller than they assumed — often because the "savings" is in a fifteen-year PPF and the current account is spent by the 25th.

When to use it

Use it without guilt when the three-part test is met. Then rebuild on the same automated schedule. The failure mode is not using the fund — it is using it for something that fails the test and then having nothing left when the real emergency arrives.

The emergency fund versus the credit card

A credit card can cover an emergency, at 36–42% a year if you cannot repay it in the interest-free window. The fund exists to make sure you never find out how expensive that is. If you have a card and no fund, the card is your emergency plan — which is why building even one month of expenses changes your financial position far more than the interest it earns.

Couples and shared funds

Two earners should agree three numbers: the monthly essentials figure, the target number of months, and which account holds the fund. Disagreement about the first usually means the fund is sized on one person's idea of essentials, and is either too small or never used.

Keep the fund in a joint account with both names, no linked debit card, and a standing instruction that tops it up monthly. Both partners should know the balance without asking — secrecy about an emergency fund defeats its purpose.

Reviewing it once a year

Expenses rise. A fund sized on 2023 spending is too small by 2026. Once a year, recalculate the monthly essentials and adjust the target. It takes ten minutes and is the difference between a fund that works and a fund that only looks like one.
