Why the government's small-savings rates change every quarter

PPF at 7.10% is not a number someone chose. It is the output of a formula — and knowing how it moves tells you when to expect the next change.

Reviewed 15 November 2026

Every quarter the Finance Ministry notifies the interest rates on small-savings schemes: PPF, Sukanya Samriddhi, National Savings Certificate, Kisan Vikas Patra, Senior Citizens' Savings Scheme, the Post Office Monthly Income Scheme and time deposits. Those rates decide what millions of households earn, and most people have no idea why they move.

The formula, in outline

Since 2016 the rates are intended to track government bond yields, with fixed spreads over the benchmark for each scheme. The idea is that a small-savings rate should not drift far from what the government itself pays to borrow. When the ten-year bond yield falls, the formula points to a lower PPF rate. When it rises, the opposite.

In practice the government retains discretion, and has often held rates steady for several quarters at a time rather than following every small move in yields. So the formula is a guide to direction, not a promise of the exact number.

Why the rates were cut through the 2016–2020 period

Because bond yields fell. The ten-year yield declined steadily through that period, and the notified rates followed with a lag — PPF moved from 8.7% to 7.1% across a handful of revisions. Nobody decided savers should earn less; the reference rate moved.

Why the quarter matters when you invest

The rate you get is the one in force when you make the deposit, not the one in force at maturity.

  • PPF and Sukanya Samriddhi apply the current rate to the balance each year. A cut affects everything you have saved so far.
  • NSC and KVP fix the rate when you buy. A certificate bought before a cut keeps its old rate for its full term — one of the few genuine advantages of these products.
  • SCSS and Post Office MIS fix the rate for the term at the time you open the account.

That difference is the practical reason the "when" of an investment matters as much as the "what" for these schemes.

What this means for a decision today

If you believe yields are heading lower, fixing a rate through NSC or SCSS protects you; PPF will follow the cuts down. If yields are heading higher, the reverse is true — a floating rate is an advantage.

Either way, the number you should act on is the one with a date next to it. That is why every rate on this site prints the quarter it applied, and links to the notification. A rate without a date is a guess about which quarter you are in.

Where to watch

Rates are notified at the end of each quarter for the next one — the dates to remember are 1 January, 1 April, 1 July and 1 October. The rates page carries the current figure for each scheme with its notification date, and every plan page repeats the date beside the rate it uses.

The eight schemes and how each one moves

The quarterly notification covers eight instruments, and they do not behave the same way:

  • PPF — floating on the balance. Rate revised quarterly; applies to everything you have saved.
  • Sukanya Samriddhi — floating on the balance. Same mechanism, higher rate.
  • NSC — fixed at purchase. A certificate bought at 7.70% keeps 7.70% for five years.
  • KVP — fixed at purchase. The doubling period is set when you buy.
  • SCSS — fixed at opening. Rate holds for the five-year term, and for any extension at the then-current rate.
  • Post Office MIS — fixed at opening. Monthly income fixed for five years.
  • Post Office Time Deposit — fixed at opening, for the term chosen.
  • Recurring Deposit — fixed at opening for each instalment in most post office variants.

That split — floating versus fixed — is the practical difference between these products when rates move, and it is the part the headline table never shows.

Why the government does not simply follow the formula

Because small-savings rates affect household income for crores of people, and because the schemes fund government borrowing. A mechanical pass-through of bond yields would have pushed PPF under 7% in 2020, which is politically and socially difficult. The result is that the notified rate often lags the formula, and sometimes holds flat for several quarters when the arithmetic would suggest a cut.

The practical lesson for a saver: watch the ten-year bond yield as a leading indicator, but do not expect an exact translation. A falling yield is a reason to consider fixing a rate; it is not a forecast of the next notification.

What this means for a portfolio

Because the floating products (PPF, SSY) respond to rate changes on the whole balance, and the fixed products (NSC, KVP, SCSS, MIS) lock at purchase, a saver worried about falling rates should weight toward the fixed ones; a saver expecting higher rates should prefer the floating ones.

Nobody can call the direction reliably. Which is why most savers are better served by holding some of each rather than betting the corpus on one view.

Questions people ask

Do rates change on 1 January, April, July and October? The notification is quarterly, but the rate does not necessarily change each time. Many notifications simply carry the previous rate forward.

Is my existing PPF balance affected by a cut? Yes. The new rate applies to the whole balance for the year, which is what "floating" means here.

Can I lock a rate before a cut? Only by buying a fixed-rate instrument — NSC, KVP, SCSS or an FD — before the notification takes effect.

Where is the notification published? In the Finance Ministry's gazette notification and on the National Savings Institute site. The rates page here carries each figure with the quarter it applied.

How a notification is actually made

The Finance Ministry issues a gazette notification, usually in the last week of the quarter, setting the rate for each small-savings instrument for the following quarter. The National Savings Institute publishes the same table for savers. Between notifications, the rate cannot change — so a rate quoted without the quarter it belongs to is ambiguous by definition.

What the bond market is telling you now

The ten-year government bond yield is the reference point. When it falls below the level implied by the current small-savings rate, the formula points to a cut; when it rises, to an increase. The government has historically moved slowly in both directions, so a large gap between the formula and the notified rate is usually closed over several quarters rather than at once.

The practical use of this: watch the yield for direction, not for the exact next number. If yields have fallen for two quarters and the small-savings rate has not moved, a cut is more likely than an increase — which is the moment to consider locking a fixed rate through NSC or SCSS.

Why the headline table is not enough

A table of rates tells you what you earn this quarter. It does not tell you whether the rate floats, when it resets, or what happens to money already deposited. Those three facts decide which product is right, and none of them appear in the table. That is the gap this site's plan pages are built to close.

The last decade, in four moves

PPF moved from 8.7% in early 2016 through a series of cuts to 7.1% by 2020, then held in a narrow band for several years while bond yields moved around it. The pattern is instructive: the rate follows the bond market slowly, in one direction, and then stays flat for long stretches.

For a saver, that means the quarter when a cut happens is worth paying attention to, and the quarters in between are not. Watching every notification is noise; watching the direction over two or three quarters is signal.

What to check each quarter

Four things, in five minutes: the PPF rate, the NSC rate, the SCSS rate, and the ten-year bond yield. If the yield has moved materially and the rates have not, the gap is either about to close or the government has chosen to hold — and either way you know more than the headline table tells you.
