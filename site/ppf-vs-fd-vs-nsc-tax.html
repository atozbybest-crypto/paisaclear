PPF vs FD vs NSC: the tax arithmetic, done properly

Three products, one headline rate each. After tax they are not the same product at all.

Reviewed 15 November 2026

Almost every comparison of PPF, fixed deposits and National Savings Certificates uses the headline rate. That number is misleading for two of the three, because they are taxed differently. Here is the arithmetic, with the assumptions stated.

The numbers used here

  • PPF: 7.10%, notified for the quarter beginning 1 October 2026 — exempt, exempt, exempt.
  • Bank FD: 7.10% assumed, for comparison. Interest taxed at your slab.
  • NSC: 7.70%, notified for the same quarter. Interest taxed at your slab.

The 4% health and education cess is added to your slab rate, so 30% becomes 31.2%, 20% becomes 20.8% and 5% becomes 5.2%. Surcharge above ₹50 lakh income is ignored here for clarity.

The only number that matters: after-tax yield

After-tax yield = headline rate × (1 − your effective slab). At the 30% slab:

PPF 7.10% × 1.00 = 7.10%
FD 7.10% × (1 − 0.312) = 4.88%
NSC 7.70% × (1 − 0.312) = 5.30%

A fixed deposit needs a headline rate of about 10.3% to match a tax-free 7.10%. That number does not exist in the Indian deposit market. This is the entire argument for PPF, and it has nothing to do with which product is "better" in the abstract.

At the 5% and 20% slabs

  • 5% slab: FD at 7.10% keeps 6.73%; NSC at 7.70% keeps 7.30%. PPF at 7.10% still wins against the FD, loses narrowly to NSC.
  • 20% slab: FD at 7.10% keeps 5.62%; NSC at 7.70% keeps 6.10%. PPF wins clearly.
  • Below the taxable threshold: no tax is due on FD interest at all. A higher-rate taxable product genuinely pays more, and this is the one case where the conventional advice reverses.

A worked example over fifteen years

₹1,50,000 a year for fifteen years, at the rates above, 30% slab:

PPF at 7.10% tax-free: about ₹40.7 lakh at maturity, tax already settled.
FD at 7.10%, compounded and taxed annually: about ₹33.1 lakh in hand after tax.
NSC at 7.70% in five-year certificates rolled over, taxed annually: about ₹35.4 lakh.

The differences are not rounding. They come from one clause in the tax code, not from the interest rate. Over fifteen years the gap between PPF and the FD is roughly ₹7.6 lakh on ₹22.5 lakh deposited.

What the comparison leaves out

Liquidity. PPF locks the money for fifteen years, with a loan from year three and partial withdrawal from year six. An FD breaks in a day with a small penalty. If you may need the money, PPF's higher after-tax return is irrelevant.

Rate revision. Neither rate is fixed for the life of the product. PPF's rate is notified quarterly and applies to the balance; an FD's rate is fixed when you book it. In a falling-rate environment the FD protects your rate; in a rising one, PPF follows it up.

The 80C illusion. All three qualify for 80C, so the deduction is identical and cancels out of the comparison. It is a reason to hold one of them, not a reason to choose between them.

Reinvestment risk on NSC. Each five-year certificate must be reinvested at whatever rate is then on offer. The 7.70% is not a fifteen-year rate; it is a five-year rate repeated three times with unknown future rates.

Three arithmetic errors people make

  • Comparing compounded FD interest with paid-out interest. An FD that pays interest out and an NSC that compounds are not comparable at the same rate. Reinvest the FD payout to compare fairly.
  • Forgetting TDS. TDS is not a final tax. If your slab is below the TDS rate, claim the refund — many savers never do.
  • Ignoring the 80C limit. The deduction is capped at ₹1.5 lakh across all instruments. Depositing ₹3 lakh into PPF does not give ₹3 lakh of deduction.

So which one

If you are in the 20% or 30% slab and will not need the money for a decade, PPF. If you need access, an FD — and accept the lower after-tax return as the price of liquidity. If you want a fixed rate rather than a floating one, NSC does that, at the cost of tax on the interest.

The same comparison at four rate combinations

Rates change, so the conclusion should not depend on one number. Here is the after-tax yield at four combinations a saver might actually face, at the 30% slab:

7.10% tax-free (PPF) → 7.10%
7.10% taxable (FD) → 4.88%
7.70% taxable (NSC) → 5.30%
8.20% taxable (SCSS, if eligible) → 5.64%

The ordering holds across every plausible rate in the current cycle. Only when the taxable rate approaches 10.3% does it overtake a 7.10% tax-free return — and no small-savings or bank product is within two percentage points of that today.

What happens if the PPF rate is cut

PPF's rate is notified quarterly and applies to the whole balance. A cut from 7.10% to 6.90% reduces the return on everything you have already saved, not just on new deposits. That is the opposite of an FD, which fixes its rate when booked, and the opposite of NSC, which fixes its rate for its five-year term.

Over a fifteen-year holding period, a 20 basis point cut compounded is not trivial: on a ₹22.5 lakh deposit schedule it is roughly ₹1.1 lakh at maturity. Which is why the "floating versus fixed" choice inside these products is a real decision, not a rounding error.

The liquidity ladder

A reasonable structure for someone in the 30% slab with a long horizon:

  1. Three to six months of expenses in a savings account or liquid fund. Not PPF. This is the emergency fund and it exists to protect the rest.
  2. PPF to the ₹1.5 lakh 80C limit, for the tax-free compounding and the 80C deduction together.
  3. NSC or a fixed deposit for money needed in three to five years, where a fixed rate matters more than the last 40 basis points.
  4. SCSS, if eligible, for the part of the corpus that must produce income and must not fall in value.

The order is deliberate: liquidity first, tax-free growth second, fixed rates for dated goals third. Reversing it is how people end up breaking a PPF in year four for a medical bill.

Questions people ask

Can I have both PPF and NSC? Yes, and many savers do. The ₹1.5 lakh 80C limit applies across both, so the deduction does not double.

Is the PPF interest really tax-free above ₹2.5 lakh a year? Contributions above ₹2.5 lakh in a financial year do not earn tax-free interest. For most savers the ₹1.5 lakh cap makes this irrelevant, but for a family pooling contributions into one account it matters.

Does a joint PPF account exist? No. One account per person. A guardian may open one for a minor.

What if I miss a year of PPF deposits? The account does not close and does not lapse, but a year without the ₹500 minimum earns no interest for that year. It can be revived with a small penalty.

Are NSC and KVP the same thing? No. NSC pays interest that accumulates to maturity over five years; KVP doubles your money over roughly 115 months with no periodic interest at all. KVP also has no 80C benefit.

The five-year comparison, in rupees

₹1,50,000 a year for five years, 30% slab, at the notified rates:

PPF at 7.10%: about ₹8.96 lakh at the end of five years, tax settled.
FD at 7.10%, interest taxed annually: about ₹8.46 lakh after tax.
NSC at 7.70%, interest taxed annually: about ₹8.58 lakh after tax.

Over five years the gap is modest — under ₹50,000. Over fifteen years it is over ₹7 lakh, because the advantage compounds. This is why the comparison matters more the longer you hold, and why a five-year view can mislead you into choosing the wrong instrument.

Three saver profiles

Salaried, 30% slab, 15-year horizon. PPF to the ₹1.5 lakh limit every year. The combination of 80C and tax-free compounding is hard to beat with anything carrying similar risk.

Freelancer, variable income, 20% slab. Keep a year of expenses liquid first. Then NSC for the fixed rate, because a fixed return matters more than the last 40 basis points when income varies.

Retiree, 65, needs income. SCSS for the guaranteed quarterly payout and the sovereign guarantee above ₹5 lakh — not PPF, which pays nothing out for fifteen years.

The mistake that costs the most

Breaking a PPF in year four for a house deposit. The penalty is the interest rate applied to the premature closure, plus the loss of the entire tax-free compounding that motivated the account. If there is any chance you need the money inside ten years, it does not belong in PPF — and that decision is made when you open the account, not when you close it.

NSC and KVP: the mechanics people mix up

NSC accumulates interest and pays it with the principal at five years. KVP pays nothing until maturity, when the amount has doubled — currently over roughly 115 months. Neither pays you anything along the way, which is why both suit money you will not touch rather than income you need.

The tax treatment differs in a way that matters: NSC qualifies for 80C and its interest is taxable as it accrues; KVP has no 80C benefit and its built-in interest is taxable at maturity. For a 30% slab saver, KVP's apparent doubling is much less impressive after tax than the brochure suggests.

Using the ₹1.5 lakh 80C limit well

The limit is shared across PPF, NSC, life insurance premiums, EPF, principal on a home loan and tuition fees. If your EPF already consumes most of it, adding PPF buys no extra deduction — only the tax-free return. That is still worth having, but it is a different reason than the one most people give.

Check your EPF contribution for the year first. Many salaried savers discover that 12% of basic plus dearness already fills a large part of the ₹1.5 lakh, and that the PPF deposit is being made for the return rather than the deduction.
