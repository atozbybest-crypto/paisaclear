How to read a health insurance policy: the five clauses that decide your claim

The premium is what you pay. These five clauses decide what you get back — and none of them are on the brochure.

Reviewed 15 November 2026

Two health policies can quote the same sum insured, the same premium and the same hospital network, and behave completely differently when you claim. The difference lives in five clauses buried in the policy wording. Read them before you pay, in this order.

This is not a comparison of insurers. It is a method for reading any policy, in about forty minutes, so that you know what you have bought before the hospital asks you to sign an admission form.

1. The waiting period — and what counts as pre-existing

Most policies exclude treatment for pre-existing conditions for two to four years. The question that matters is not the number of years but what the insurer counts as pre-existing: a condition diagnosed before the policy started, or one whose symptoms existed even if it was never diagnosed.

A policy that defines pre-existing as "diagnosed or treated" is safer than one that says "diagnosed, treated, or for which medical advice was sought". The second version lets an insurer argue that a cough you mentioned to a doctor in 2021 was an undeclared condition.

Also look for the specific waiting periods on named procedures. Cataract, hernia, joint replacement and maternity usually have their own clocks — often longer than the general pre-existing period. A policy with a two-year pre-existing wait can still have a four-year wait on knee replacement.

What to do: search the wording for "waiting period" and read every mention, not just the first. There are usually three: the initial waiting period (30 days for everything except accidents), the specific-disease waiting period, and the pre-existing waiting period. They run in parallel and the longest one applies.

2. Room rent limits — the clause that costs the most

A policy capping room rent at 1% of sum insured per day does more damage than people expect. When your room exceeds the cap, most insurers apply a proportionate deduction to the entire bill, not just the room charge.

On a ₹5 lakh cover, a 1% cap is ₹5,000 a day. Admit yourself to an ₹8,000 room and the insurer may reduce the whole claim by the same ratio — paying 62% of the surgeon's fee, the ICU charge, the medicines, the lot. The clause with the smallest print has the largest effect on what you receive.

Single private room without a cap is worth paying for. If the choice is ₹4 lakh with no room cap or ₹6 lakh with a 1% cap, the smaller policy with no cap often settles more.

The arithmetic, worked: a ₹3 lakh bill, of which ₹40,000 is the room. With no cap, the insurer pays close to the lot. With a 1% cap on ₹5 lakh (₹5,000 a day) and an eight-day stay at ₹8,000, the allowed room rent is ₹40,000 against ₹64,000 billed — a ratio of 62%. Applied proportionately across the bill, the insurer pays roughly ₹1.87 lakh and you pay ₹1.13 lakh. The room cost you ₹1.13 lakh of your own money, not ₹24,000.

3. Sub-limits on specific treatments

A ₹10 lakh policy does not mean ₹10 lakh for everything. Cataract surgery may be capped at ₹40,000 per eye; maternity at ₹50,000; cardiac procedures at a percentage of sum insured. These are separate ceilings inside the main one.

Ask for the sub-limit table in writing. If an insurer will not give it before you buy, that is your answer. The table usually sits in the policy schedule, not the wording, and it is the single document most people never read.

4. Co-payment and deductible

A co-payment is the share of every claim you pay — 10% or 20% is common in senior-citizen policies. A deductible is a fixed amount you pay before cover starts, for example the first ₹25,000 each year.

Neither is unreasonable on its own, and both reduce the premium. But they compound: a 20% co-payment plus a ₹25,000 deductible on a ₹2 lakh bill leaves you paying ₹65,000. Work that arithmetic with your own likely bill before choosing the cheaper premium.

A useful rule: never accept a co-payment above 10% unless the alternative is no cover at all. Co-payment is permanent; a premium saving is not.

5. Restoration and recharge

Restoration puts the sum insured back after you have used it, usually once a year and usually only for a different illness. Some policies restore automatically and without limit; others restore only once, and only if the second claim is unrelated to the first.

For a family floater, this clause matters more than the headline sum insured. One hospitalisation can exhaust a ₹5 lakh cover; whether it comes back decides if the policy is useful for the rest of the year.

Beyond the five: four more things worth finding

  • Day-care procedures. Cataract, dialysis, chemotherapy and many others no longer need a night's stay. Confirm they are covered without hospitalisation.
  • Ambulance and consumables. Consumables — gloves, syringes, implants — are often excluded or capped. On a large surgery this is a real bill.
  • Pre and post hospitalisation. 30 and 60 days is standard; 90 and 180 is better, and it covers the diagnostics before and after admission.
  • Network hospitals. Check the insurer's list yourself, for your city and your preferred hospital, rather than asking the agent.

A forty-minute reading routine

Search the PDF for these words in order: waiting, room, sub-limit, co-pay, restoration, exclusion. Read the paragraph before and after each hit. That gives you a working understanding of the policy in under an hour, and it is more than most buyers ever do.

What to do with this

Ask for the policy wording — not the brochure — before you buy, and search it for four words: waiting, room, sub-limit, co-pay. Those four sections take twenty minutes to read and decide whether the policy pays when you need it.

The health insurance records on this site list what each insurer says about its own plan, with the date it was checked. They are a starting point for that reading, not a substitute for it.

Six claims, and which clause decides each one

The clauses only matter in combination, and the combination is easier to see in examples. These six are ordinary Indian hospital situations.

A cataract operation, day care, ₹55,000 billed. The policy has a ₹40,000 sub-limit per eye. The insurer pays ₹40,000; you pay ₹15,000 plus any consumables excluded. No waiting period applies if the policy is older than the specified-disease wait.

A knee replacement, ₹4.2 lakh, in a ₹6,000 room. The cap is 1% of ₹5 lakh, which is ₹5,000 a day. On a six-day stay the room is over by ₹6,000, and because the room exceeded the cap, the insurer applies proportionate deduction to the whole bill: it pays roughly 83%, or ₹3.5 lakh. You pay ₹70,000, of which only ₹6,000 was the actual room overrun.

A cardiac stent at 14 months, with a policy bought at 62. The pre-existing wait is three years and the insured had hypertension declared at proposal. The claim is examined for whether the condition existed before the policy. If the insurer links it, the claim is rejected in full — with the premium refunded. This is why the definition of pre-existing is the single most important sentence in the document.

A second hospitalisation in the same year, same family floater. The sum insured is exhausted by the first. Restoration decides whether the second claim is paid at all, and most policies restore only for a different illness, once a year.

Maternity, planned, in year two. A two-year waiting period applies. The claim falls inside the wait and is excluded, even though the policy is "two years old" by calendar. Waiting periods run from the policy start date, not from the date of the last renewal.

Dengue, five days, ₹90,000, first month of a new policy. The initial waiting period of 30 days excludes everything except accidents. The claim is rejected — a fact that surprises people annually during monsoon.

Questions people ask after reading the wording

Does a higher sum insured fix a room-rent cap? Sometimes. If the cap is expressed as a percentage of sum insured, doubling the cover doubles the allowed room rent. If it is a fixed rupee amount, it does not move.

Is a 30-day initial waiting period negotiable? Not usually, but accidents are covered from day one in most policies. Read whether the policy separates accident cover from illness cover.

What happens if I switch insurers? Waiting periods are not always carried forward. Some insurers credit the previous policy's continuity if you switch without a gap, some do not. Ask specifically, and get it in writing before you move.

Do I need a separate critical illness policy? Different product. Health indemnity pays hospital bills; critical illness pays a lump sum on diagnosis. The second covers income loss and non-medical costs, and it does not replace the first.

Is a top-up worth it? A top-up begins paying after a deductible, which is usually satisfied by the base policy's cover. It is a cheap way to raise total protection, provided the deductible matches what the base policy actually pays.

A worked claim, start to finish

A 41-year-old with a ₹8 lakh family floater, bought four years ago, admitted for gallbladder surgery. Bill: ₹2.6 lakh, five nights, single private room at ₹9,000 a night. The policy has no room cap, a ₹50,000 sub-limit on laparoscopic surgery, and 60 days of post-hospitalisation cover.

The insurer pays the room in full. It pays the surgery within the sub-limit, so ₹50,000 of the ₹1.1 lakh surgical charge is covered and ₹60,000 falls to the insured. Consumables of ₹18,000 are excluded. The final settlement is about ₹1.8 lakh, and the family pays ₹80,000.

Nothing here was a surprise to a reader of the wording. The sub-limit and the consumables exclusion were both in the schedule, in plain text. This is the ordinary shape of a "covered" claim: the insurer pays most of it, and the insured pays a share that was knowable in advance.

Five myths worth killing

  • "Pre-existing means diagnosed." Most wordings include symptoms and advice sought, not just diagnosis.
  • "A higher sum insured means more cover for each thing." Sub-limits and caps are per treatment, not per policy size.
  • "Cashless means free." Cashless means the insurer settles with the hospital; exclusions and co-pay are still yours.
  • "The agent knows the policy." The agent knows the brochure. The wording is the contract.
  • "Claim rejection means the insurer cheated." Most rejections trace to non-disclosure or a waiting period that was printed and unread.

A checklist you can carry to the counter

Ask for, in writing, before paying: the policy wording PDF, the schedule of sub-limits, the list of waiting periods by condition, the co-payment clause, the network hospital list for your city, and the claim settlement ratio for the last three years. Six documents, all of which the insurer already has.

At the hospital counter

Three things decide how smoothly a cashless claim goes, and all three are in your control. Pre-authorisation should be filed before a planned admission, not on the day. The hospital's insurance desk needs the policy number, the insured's ID and the treating doctor's estimate. Ask for the pre-auth approval letter in writing — the amount approved, not just "approved".

During the stay, ask for a daily bill. Disputes are far easier to settle while you are in the building than after discharge. And at discharge, do not sign the final settlement until the deductions are explained line by line; the hospital will usually have the insurer's deduction sheet.

The renewal checklist, once a year

Renewal is the moment the policy can quietly change. Check five things each year: the sum insured, the premium, whether any waiting period was reset, whether the network list shrank, and whether new exclusions were added to the wording. Insurers do revise wordings at renewal, and an older policy sometimes has better terms than a newer one — which is a reason to stay rather than switch.

Put the renewal date in your calendar a month ahead, set it to autopay, and keep the receipt with the proposal form. A lapsed policy at 60 is a far more expensive problem than a slightly higher premium.
